BRIEF I
01 / Pedophilia

Minors, Marriage, and the Failure to Protect

DOCUMENTED
ArgumentPrimary referencesSecondary referencesPrecision & response

The Church's own history admits that its founder married a child.

The Gospel Topics essay "Plural Marriage in Kirtland and Nauvoo," published in October 2014, estimates that Joseph Smith was sealed to between 30 and 40 women, and states that the youngest, Helen Mar Kimball, was sealed to him several months before her 15th birthday.1 Helen was born on August 22, 1828, and sealed in 1843: she was 14. Joseph Smith was 37. The essay also acknowledges sealings to women already married to living husbands; historian Todd Compton counts eleven of them — fully a third of the total.9 The essay's explanation, that such a marriage was "inappropriate by today's standards" but legal at the time, concedes the point even in the Church's chosen framing.

The practice did not end with Joseph. Brigham Young married Clarissa (Clara) Decker on May 8, 1844. The Church Historian's Press records her birth as July 22, 1828 — making her 15 at the wedding. Young was 42.10 As plural marriage took shape, girls under 16 could be given to the most powerful men in the movement, often with a parent's encouragement; Helen Mar Kimball's own account of her father offering her to the Prophet is the best-known example.

The modern question is whether the institution now puts children ahead of its own legal exposure. The Paul Adams case in Bisbee, Arizona, suggests it did not. According to court records and the Associated Press, Adams, a U.S. Border Patrol agent, confessed to Bishop John Herrod in 2011 that he was sexually abusing his 5-year-old daughter. Herrod, also a family physician, followed Church policy and called the Church's abuse help line. In a recorded interview with federal investigators, he said he was told: "You absolutely can do nothing."2 Church lawyers have stated in the litigation that every help-line call Herrod and his successor made was taken by attorneys at Kirton McConkie, the Church's Salt Lake City law firm. In 2012 Herrod told the incoming bishop, Robert "Kim" Mauzy, who held a disciplinary council and excommunicated Adams in 2013. No one notified police or child-welfare authorities.

The abuse went on for about seven years and spread to a younger daughter. A federal agent testified that the videos showed her abuse began when she was roughly three to six weeks old. Adams recorded the abuse and shared it online. In 2016 New Zealand police found a video and started an international search; on February 8, 2017, Homeland Security agents arrested Adams at the Naco border crossing and rescued all six children.7 He was indicted on federal charges and died by suicide in custody in December 2017, before trial. His wife, Leizza, pleaded no contest to two counts of child abuse and was sentenced to 2½ years in prison followed by probation.

The AP's 2022 investigation drew on nearly 12,000 pages of sealed records from an unrelated West Virginia lawsuit. They show that the help line, set up in 1995, sorts calls through a written protocol whose "High Risk Cases" section asks about the Church's legal exposure, sends serious cases to Kirton McConkie lawyers, and — Axios reported — destroys call records.2,6 The AP concluded that the system can easily be misused to steer abuse reports away from law enforcement. The Church says the help line exists to protect victims and to help bishops follow reporting laws.

The courts have now closed the door on the Adams children. A Cochise County judge dismissed their lawsuit in November 2023; the Court of Appeals revived it. On July 30, 2026, the Arizona Supreme Court ruled unanimously for the Church, holding that the state's mandatory-reporting statute, A.R.S. § 13-3620, lets clergy withhold confessional communications when they judge it "reasonable and necessary within the concepts of the religion," and that First Amendment principles bar judges and juries from second-guessing a church's view of what counts as a confession.3,4 The plaintiffs' lawyer, Lynne Cadigan, called it a shocking decision and moved for reconsideration.5

The legislature has not filled the gap. Bills to require clergy to report ongoing abuse were introduced by Sen. Victoria Steele from 2021 to 2023 and by Rep. Stacey Travers from 2023 through 2026 (HB 2039 in 2026). Each time, committee chairs refused them a hearing.8 The case shows a system in which the Church's lawyers, its doctrine, and state law together kept a known abuser's secret while two girls were abused.

BRIEF II
02 / Financial Corruption

A $100-Billion-Plus Question of Trust

UNAUDITED
ArgumentPrimary referencesSecondary referencesPrecision & response

Members give a tenth of their income. In the United States, they cannot see where it goes.

Being a full-tithe payer is one of the temple-recommend interview questions, so tithing is a condition of entering the temple — the only place the Church teaches that marriages can be sealed for eternity. From 1915 to 1959 the Church read a detailed financial statement at its April General Conference. As tax scholar Samuel Brunson documents, it stopped after heavy deficit spending and investment losses in 1959.4 Since then U.S. members have heard only a one-paragraph Auditing Department statement that funds were handled properly. Where the law requires it, the Church does file accounts: its British charity is audited and registered with the Charity Commission, and it files public returns in Canada and Australia.10

What U.S. members know comes largely from a whistleblower and a regulator. In December 2019 David Nielsen, a former portfolio manager at Ensign Peak Advisors, filed an IRS whistleblower complaint alleging that the Church's investment arm held about $100 billion it rarely used for charity, and that Ensign Peak money helped bail out a Church insurer (Beneficial Life) and fund the City Creek Center mall — about $1.4 billion by his count — despite Church statements that City Creek was built without tithing. Independent estimates have since gone far past that figure. The Widow's Mite Report, as summarized by the Salt Lake Tribune, estimates total Church investments at about $231 billion and total wealth at about $321 billion at the end of 2025.3,5 The Church's directly held U.S. stock portfolio, disclosed to the SEC, reached a high of $60.9 billion in the third quarter of 2025.7

The SEC's findings are the most important evidence. On February 21, 2023, the Commission found that from 1997 to 2019 Ensign Peak, with the Church's knowledge, failed to file required Forms 13F in its own name, filing instead through 13 shell LLCs that misstated who controlled the investments. The order says the Church feared that disclosing its portfolio — about $32 billion by 2018 — would bring negative consequences, and that senior leaders approved additional shell companies in 2011 and 2015, fronted by managers chosen to be hard to link to the Church. Ensign Peak paid a $4 million penalty and the Church $1 million.1 A Church that taught openness about tithing chose, at its highest level, to hide its size from regulators for 22 years.

The lawsuits have not gone the members' way. James Huntsman, brother of former Utah Gov. Jon Huntsman Jr., sued in 2021 to recover more than $5 million in tithing, alleging misrepresentation about City Creek's funding. A Ninth Circuit panel revived the case 2–1 in 2023, but on January 31, 2025, an 11-judge en banc court ruled unanimously for the Church, finding that no reasonable juror could conclude the Church had misrepresented the source of City Creek's money, since it had said the project used earnings on invested reserves.2,8 Five judges would also have barred the suit on church-autonomy grounds; the majority declined to go that far. Abroad, a 2022 investigation by The Sydney Morning Herald, The Age, 60 Minutes, and the CBC questioned the management of Australian tax-deductible funds and the transfer of more than $1 billion in Canadian tax-exempt donations to BYU campuses in the U.S.9

The cost falls heavily on the young. Young men may begin missions at 18 and serve 24 months; since November 21, 2025, young women may also begin at 18, serving 18 months.11 U.S. and Canadian missionaries and their families pay a flat monthly amount into ward funds. Meanwhile the institution holds reserves that, by independent analysis, could fund Church programs indefinitely even if donations stopped.6

BRIEF III
03 / Chauvinism

A Patriarchy by Design

MALE-ONLY
ArgumentPrimary referencesSecondary referencesPrecision & response

The Church's governing structure is male by rule, not by chance.

Every presiding office — President of the Church, apostle, Seventy, stake president, bishop — is held only by ordained men, and women cannot be ordained to the priesthood. The Relief Society, founded in Nauvoo in 1842, is one of the largest women's organizations in the world, and women lead it and the Young Women and Primary organizations. But they serve under priesthood keys held by men. A bishop, not a woman, conducts the worthiness interviews of girls and women, hears their confessions, and holds membership councils about them.

Until recently, the hierarchy was written into the Church's most sacred ritual. Under the 1990 revision of the temple endowment, women covenanted to hearken to the counsel of their husbands while husbands covenanted directly with God — itself a softening of an earlier promise to obey the law of the husband. In January 2019 the Church removed the hearken language, gave Eve more speaking lines, and ended the veiling of women's faces; men and women now make the same covenants.1,4 Faithful Latter-day Saint feminists welcomed the change. It also confirms what critics had long said: for nearly thirty years, the temple taught women to reach God through their husbands. Further revisions followed in 2023, and commentators still point to gendered wording in the sealing ceremony.5

The Church has disciplined those who pressed these questions publicly. Kate Kelly, a human-rights lawyer, founded Ordain Women in 2013. On June 23, 2014, her Virginia ward's disciplinary council excommunicated her in absentia for conduct contrary to the laws and order of the Church; her bishop wrote that she was not entitled to promote her views or recruit others to them.2,8 In 2015 the Church published an essay affirming a divine Mother in Heaven, while stating that no revelation on the subject has been recorded and not authorizing prayer to her.9

The same structure governs children. For decades, bishops — untrained lay men — interviewed boys and girls from about age 12, alone and behind closed doors, about worthiness, including questions about masturbation and sexual behavior. Former bishop Sam Young launched Protect LDS Children in 2018, gathered thousands of accounts of harm, led hundreds of people to Church headquarters to deliver them, and staged a 23-day hunger strike under the demand "No one-on-one interviews."6 In 2018 the Church updated its guidelines to allow a parent or another adult to be present; Young called this inadequate. On September 16, 2018, he announced that a Houston disciplinary council had excommunicated him for conduct contrary to the laws and order of the Church; his appeal was rejected that November.3,7 The Church revised youth-interview guidance and temple-recommend questions again in 2019. Two reformers were expelled, and parts of their reforms were then adopted — a pattern that shows how dissent is treated when it comes from outside the male line of authority.

The evidence suggests this matters to members. Jana Riess's research, including a 2026 analysis titled "Put on a pedestal, kept out of power," cites Pew data showing retention of those raised in the Church falling from 70% (2007) to 64% (2014) to 54% in Pew's most recent study, and her survey found that 21% of former members named the role of women as a reason for leaving.10,11 The pattern is consistent: change comes slowly, from the top, after outside pressure. Women can be consulted. They cannot preside.

Precision notes & the Church's response

  • Interview reforms. The 2018 guidelines allowed a parent or another adult to be present; they did not end one-on-one interviews or every sensitive question. Current guidance is in the Church's General Handbook.

  • The Church's position. Leaders teach that men and women are equal partners with different roles, and that priesthood power — though not priesthood office — is available to women through temple covenants.

BRIEF IV
04 / Psychological Warfare

The Architecture of Persuasion

BY DESIGN
ArgumentPrimary referencesSecondary referencesPrecision & response

Every church persuades. This one codifies the method.

Preach My Gospel, the missionary manual first issued in 2004 and revised in 2023, trains 18- and 19-year-old missionaries to extend specific invitations to investigators — read the Book of Mormon, pray about it, attend church, keep commandments, set a baptismal date — to attach a promised blessing to each, and to follow up on every one.2 Critics call this the commitment pattern. Its structure, a sequence of small, public commitments escalating toward baptism, matches the consistency-based persuasion long described in social psychology.11

The test of truth missionaries teach is emotional rather than evidential. Moroni 10:4–5 promises that a sincere person who prays will learn the Book of Mormon's truth by the power of the Holy Ghost; Church teaching often describes that witness in the "burning in the bosom" language of D&C 9:8. Investigators learn to treat a warm, peaceful feeling as confirmation — and to treat later doubts as spiritual danger rather than new information.

Apostle Boyd K. Packer stated the method plainly. In "The Candle of the Lord," a June 25, 1982 address to new mission presidents published in the Ensign in January 1983, he taught: "A testimony is to be found in the bearing of it!" Answering missionaries who worried that testifying without a testimony would be dishonest, he urged them to step into the darkness and speak first.1 Apostles David A. Bednar and Dallin H. Oaks have repeated the idea that testimonies are gained in the act of bearing them.6,7

Leaving carries steep costs. Members who resign or are disciplined lose temple access, which until 2019 also determined who could watch a family wedding. Mixed-faith families, mission companions, ward networks — and in Utah, often employers and neighbors — are woven into a single community. Leaders have long warned members away from critical material, keeping doubters from the information that might resolve their questions. Critics, many of them former members, apply cult researcher Steven Hassan's BITE model — Behavior, Information, Thought, and Emotional control — to these practices.9

The Church has acknowledged that its own narrative needed correcting. Between 2013 and 2015 it published Gospel Topics essays conceding Joseph Smith's polyandry, his use of a seer stone in a hat to translate, multiple First Vision accounts, and the racist roots of the priesthood ban. Many members first learned these facts from the essays, often years after baptism, and former members interviewed by Riess ranked doubts about Church history among their top reasons for leaving.4

Retention is falling. Pew found that 70% of those raised Latter-day Saint still identified with the Church as adults in 2007, 64% in 2014, and 54% in its latest study; the General Social Survey puts millennial retention at 46%.3,5 The evidence suggests that persuasion designed to produce commitment before full information works well enough to baptize people — and that many of them leave once they learn more.

  • The Church's position. Its teaching also calls for study alongside prayer, and apologists argue Packer was describing faith that precedes evidence, not dishonesty. Current Church messaging emphasizes seeking truth and links directly to the Gospel Topics essays.

BRIEF V
05 / Racism

The Ban and the Folklore

1852–1978
ArgumentPrimary referencesSecondary referencesPrecision & response

For 126 years, the Church barred men of Black African descent from the priesthood — and Black members from the temple.

The restriction was not there from the start. Elijah Abel, a Black convert, was ordained an elder in 1836 and later a Seventy with Joseph Smith's approval; Q. Walker Lewis was ordained an elder in the 1840s. The Church's own 2013 essay "Race and the Priesthood" acknowledges this, and states that in 1852 Brigham Young publicly announced that men of Black African descent could no longer be ordained.1 The ban was a decision made by a leader, not a doctrine revealed from the beginning.

Leaders then built justifications on top of it. Brigham Young tied the ban to the curse of Cain; later leaders added that Black people had been less valiant in a premortal life. On August 17, 1949, the First Presidency declared the restriction a direct commandment from the Lord rather than a policy.6 In 1954 Apostle Mark E. Petersen defended segregation before BYU religion teachers, asking who man was to change a segregation the Lord had made.7 Bruce R. McConkie's widely used Mormon Doctrine (1958) taught that Black people were ineligible because of premortal conduct.8 The First Presidency reaffirmed the ban in 1969, at the height of the civil-rights movement.

Pressure mounted as the Church's reputation suffered. In 1968 the UTEP track team protested BYU; in October 1969 Wyoming's "Black 14" were dismissed from their football team for planning to wear armbands against BYU; in November 1969 Stanford announced it would schedule no new contests with BYU, and in 1970 the University of Washington moved to cut ties.9 In Brazil, where ancestry is often mixed and hard to trace, the Church was building the São Paulo temple, announced in 1975. On June 8, 1978, President Spencer W. Kimball announced the revelation now canonized as Official Declaration 2, extending the priesthood to all worthy male members without regard to race or color.2 Months later, McConkie told religion teachers to forget whatever he had said that conflicted with the new revelation.

The Church never apologized. In December 2013 its essay disavowed "the theories advanced in the past" that Black skin signaled divine disfavor or premortal unrighteousness, that mixed-race marriage was sinful, or that any race was inferior.1 It does not name Young, the 1949 First Presidency, Petersen, or McConkie, and it does not call the ban itself an error. Historian Lester Bush's 1973 Dialogue article first traced the ban's documentary origins to Brigham Young's era, and is widely credited with preparing leaders for the 1978 change.3 W. Paul Reeve's Religion of a Different Color shows how the ban grew out of nineteenth-century efforts to make Latter-day Saints appear white.4

The Church has since pursued reconciliation. Its leaders met NAACP leadership in May 2018, and in June 2021 announced $2 million a year in humanitarian aid and $1 million a year in UNCF-administered scholarships, each for three years, plus a fellowship named for the Rev. Amos C. Brown.10,11 These are real steps. But they sit beside a record in which the Church's highest councils called a racial exclusion God's command for more than a century — and whose official repudiation still blames unnamed "theories" rather than the prophets who taught them.

Precision notes & the Church's response

  • Scope of the ban. Black members could be baptized and confirmed. They were barred from priesthood ordination (men) and from the endowment and sealing (men and women), though some proxy baptisms were permitted.
  • Who was covered. The ban applied to men of Black African descent; a 1907 rule applied it to anyone with known African ancestry. Pacific Islanders and other groups were not covered.
  • Dates. The campus protests ran 1968–1970. The São Paulo temple was announced in 1975 and dedicated October 30, 1978; historians debate how much Brazil shaped the timing.
  • The disavowal repudiates the theories, not the ban itself, and names no leaders.
  • The Church's position. The essay condemns all racism, past and present. Leaders point to the 1978 revelation, the NAACP partnership, and rapid growth in Africa as evidence of change.

BRIEF VI
06 / Arrogance

The Doctrine of Exclusivity

MEMBERS ONLY
ArgumentPrimary referencesSecondary referencesPrecision & response

The Church's founding scripture claims sole legitimacy — and that claim reaches into every family, living and dead.

Doctrine and Covenants 1:30, an 1831 revelation that prefaces the book, calls it "the only true and living church upon the face of the whole earth."1 This is current teaching, repeated by missionaries and in monthly testimony meetings. In Joseph Smith's 1838 account of the First Vision, Christ says of other churches that they were all wrong and their creeds an abomination.9 By the Church's own scripture, every other Christian tradition lacks authority to perform valid saving ordinances.

The doctrine lands directly on families. D&C 131:1–4 teaches that the highest degree of the celestial kingdom requires the new and everlasting covenant of marriage, and D&C 132 teaches that marriages not sealed by that authority end at death. A loving marriage in any other church, or a civil one, is therefore taught to be temporary. Only members with a current temple recommend may enter a temple — so for generations, non-member parents, less-active siblings, and members who had not paid full tithing waited in the lobby while their children married.

The Church added a penalty to that exclusion. Until 2019, in the United States and many other countries, a couple who married civilly first — often so excluded relatives could attend — had to wait a year before being sealed. On May 6, 2019, the First Presidency ended the waiting period, saying it would let families come together in love and unity under a single global standard.2,4 The change shows the Church understood the pain it caused. Couples nonetheless spent decades choosing between including their parents and being sealed.

Exclusivity extends to the dead. Latter-day Saints perform proxy baptisms so the deceased may accept the gospel in the afterlife. After Jewish organizations objected to the baptism of Holocaust victims, the Church agreed in 1995 to remove their names unless they were direct ancestors of living members. The practice continued. A renewed 2010 agreement added computer screening. In February 2012, researcher Helen Radkey documented the proxy baptism of Nazi hunter Simon Wiesenthal's parents; Anne Frank was baptized by proxy again in the Dominican Republic, and journalist Daniel Pearl was also baptized.5,6,7 The Church apologized and suspended the submitter's access, and on March 2, 2012 the First Presidency told members that, without exception, they must not submit the names of celebrities or Jewish Holocaust victims.3,8 The Simon Wiesenthal Center said the incidents made a mockery of years of meetings.

The Church presents these teachings as an offer, not an imposition: proxy ordinances may be accepted or rejected by the deceased, and its claim to unique authority, it says, is compatible with respect for others. But a theology that calls other churches' creeds an abomination, marriages outside the temple temporary, and the dead in need of its rites will be experienced as arrogant by many outside it — and the repeated proxy baptisms of Holocaust victims, after promises made in 1995 and 2010, show the limits of the Church's ability or willingness to police that impulse.

Precision notes & the Church's response

  • The one-year rule was not universal. Where law required a civil ceremony first, the Church allowed immediate sealing. Since May 6, 2019, couples anywhere may marry civilly and be sealed immediately. A one-year wait after baptism still applies to new converts.
  • Exclusion from the sealing continues. Family members without a recommend still cannot attend the sealing itself, though a civil ceremony beforehand is now easier.
  • Proxy baptisms. The 1995 agreement and 2010 renewal are documented. The 2012 cases were submissions by individual members in violation of policy; the Church apologized and restricted database access. Anne Frank had been baptized by proxy several times before 2012.
  • A characterization, not a finding. Many faiths make exclusive truth claims. D&C 1:30 is quoted here in the Church's own words so readers can judge.
  • The Church's position. Proxy ordinances do not make the dead members and are offered for them to accept or reject; "only true church" refers to priesthood authority, not to the moral worth of others.
